What they actually ran

- LLM judge: Haiku 4.5, no reasoning, single sample, binary output, sees full prompt + response with an instruction to distrust comments. Primed with the two known hack modes (overwriting the eval function, hardcoding test answers). 67% accuracy on hacks held-out (84% on hacks-or-attempts), collapsing to ~50% during training — but recall held at ~85-95%. So the collapse is a precision collapse on on-policy data (long responses, unfaithful comments, tests that can't raise), not a recall failure. Even at ~50% accuracy the penalty still protected.
- Linear probe: logistic regression on layer-34 base-model activations, ROC AUC 0.99, threshold at 5% FPR, ~80% accuracy during training. Penalty → 0% hacking.
- Ground-truth monitor: literally our arb-pass/self-pass logic (does the code pass its own run_tests but not the real tests). Degraded 90%/70% variants made by uniform label flipping — and the 90% variant's penalty still ended at 15% hacking, worse than the 80%-accurate probe.
- Application: penalty = a negative reward term equal in magnitude to the correctness reward, sequence-level; screening = flagged rollouts dropped from the gradient entirely. Penalty ~0%, screening 0.9% with a slow uptick.

How reliable is the judge, and is ~100% TP the right target?

Their judge is mediocre as a classifier and it still worked — and their 90%-GT result shows a better classifier failing. Those two facts together say scalar accuracy is the wrong axis, and I'd push back on aiming at "near-100% TP rate" as the goal:

1. Consistency beats accuracy. The 80% probe beat the 90% ground-truth-with-flipped-labels because uniform noise lets a consistent hack shape through 10% of the time, and reinforcement ratchets — any surviving credit compounds. The probe's errors are structured: it reliably catches the attractor shape and misses weird periphery. A judge's error model matters more than its error rate. Your near-100% judge only helps if its residual misses are not concentrated on one reachable shape.
2. In this environment, near-100% TP on the shapes that matter is free and exact — no judge needed. Everything heritable is a cannot-fail grader, and that's an AST check plus (to be airtight) executing the grader against an arbitrary solution. That's the project's queued trajectory filter, which is exactly their screening intervention with a deterministic, 100%-recall, 0%-FPR monitor for bucket 1. 006 gives it a strong prior: when cannot-fail shapes can't be sampled, bucket 3 alone doesn't compound — so removing them from the gradient instead should work too. An LLM judge in this env is answering a different question: what does monitoring look like when you can't write the exact detector — and improving its prompt with our env knowledge partially un-asks that question.
3. The realistic frontier is FPR under distribution shift, not TP. Their recall was already 85-95%; what collapsed was precision, and with penalty = the full correctness reward, every false positive on an honest rollout is a large wrong gradient. Our runs sharpen why that's expensive: late airtight batches are ~90% honest asserting graders, many failing legitimately — precisely the "test that fails" surface their judge misfires on. If you build the better judge, I'd measure FP rate on honest-assert rollouts from our dumps (we have 153k labeled ones sitting in the cache) before believing any accuracy number from a held-out pre-training set — their own 67%→50% gap is the cost of skipping that.

So my recommendation, Tetlock-mode: a Haiku-class judge with a prompt informed by the ladder (name the guard, uncalled-nested, no-assert, rebind shapes; tell it to check whether asserts can execute) plausibly gets >95% recall / <2% FPR on our dumps — I'd put ~0.7 on that. But the decision-relevant experiment isn't the ceiling judge; it's holding the judge realistic (no loophole foreknowledge) and asking whether structured-but-imperfect beats uniform-noise-at-higher-accuracy, which their probe-vs-90%-GT pair only hints at with n=1 arms. That's the gap in their benchmark our per-shape labels could actually fill — and note their monitors also cost real money at 51,200 rollouts per run, which is why they ran Haiku without reasoning, single-sample.
